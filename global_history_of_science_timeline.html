Ancient world — foundations
~300 BCEGreece / Alexandria
Systematic observation and logical proof
Aristotle, Euclid, Archimedes
Aristotle developed the idea that knowledge of the natural world should come from careful observation — not just from reasoning alone. Euclid formalised mathematical proof. Archimedes built physical experiments. Together they established the idea that claims about nature can be tested and demonstrated.
"The roots of education are bitter, but the fruit is sweet." — Aristotle
But note the limit: Aristotle still believed in reasoning from first principles — if the logic was right, the conclusion must be true, regardless of what observation showed. It would take another thousand years to fully break from this.

~600 BCE – 500 CEIndia
Rigorous logic, mathematics and the concept of zero
Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, the Nyaya school
Indian scholars developed sophisticated systems of logic and epistemology (theories of how we know things) centuries before the European Enlightenment. The Nyaya school asked: what counts as valid evidence? How do we distinguish perception, inference and testimony? Brahmagupta formalised the concept of zero and negative numbers — without which modern mathematics and computing are impossible. Aryabhata calculated the Earth's circumference and proposed heliocentrism a thousand years before Copernicus.
"Just as a lamp illuminates a room, mathematical knowledge illuminates the mind." — Aryabhata
Connection to modern science: The decimal number system used in all modern science — including genetics statistics and population biology — is of Indian origin, transmitted to Europe via the Islamic world.

~200 BCE – 1000 CEChina
Technology, empiricism and practical knowledge
Various — no single author tradition
Chinese science was characterised by deep empirical observation in service of practical ends — agriculture, medicine, engineering. Paper, printing, gunpowder and the compass were all Chinese innovations that transformed the world. Chinese medicine developed sophisticated observational traditions — including inoculation against smallpox centuries before Jenner. The Needham Question — "why did modern science emerge in Europe and not China?" — remains one of the most debated questions in the history of science.
On the Needham Question: China had superior technology for centuries. Some historians argue the absence of a culture of public debate and peer challenge — where others could scrutinise and falsify your claims — meant knowledge accumulated without the self-correcting mechanism that Popper later identified as essential. This is contested but worth discussing.

750 – 1258 CE — the Islamic Golden Age
~830 CEBaghdad — House of Wisdom
The world's first research institution
Caliph al-Ma'mun — Bayt al-Hikma
The House of Wisdom in Baghdad was the intellectual centre of the world for over 400 years. Scholars translated Greek, Indian and Persian texts — then went far beyond them. The institution brought together Muslims, Christians, Jews and scholars of many backgrounds. It was explicitly multicultural and multilingual. Science was understood as a religious duty — the Quran's repeated exhortations to observe, reflect and reason were taken seriously as a mandate for inquiry.
"Seek knowledge, even unto China." — Hadith attributed to the Prophet Muhammad
Why this matters: The Islamic world did not merely preserve Greek knowledge — it systematically extended and corrected it. When European universities emerged in the 12th century, their curricula were built largely on Islamic scholarship, often still in Arabic.

780 – 850 CEBaghdad
Algebra and the algorithm — the language of modern genetics
Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi
Al-Khwarizmi's work Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala gave us the word algebra (from al-jabr). The word algorithm comes from the Latinisation of his own name. His systematic approach to solving equations created the mathematical framework that underpins modern computing, statistical genetics, bioinformatics and DNA sequencing analysis. Every time a genome is sequenced, algorithm-driven mathematics — rooted in his work — is doing the processing.
Direct relevance: Modern genetics is impossible without the mathematics al-Khwarizmi formalised. The sequencing of the human genome required algorithms running on computers. The connection from 9th century Baghdad to 21st century gene editing is direct and traceable.
algebraalgorithmfoundations of computing
965 – 1040 CEBasra / Cairo
The experimental method — 600 years before Bacon
Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen)
Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics is one of the most important scientific works ever written — not primarily for what it discovered about light and vision, but for how it discovered it. He explicitly rejected the Greek method of reasoning from first principles. Instead he insisted: hypotheses must be tested through controlled experiment. Observations must be repeatable. The experimenter must control for variables. He documented his methods so others could reproduce his results. This is Popper's scientific method, implemented 900 years early.
"The seeker after truth is not one who studies the writings of the ancients and, following his natural disposition, puts his trust in them — but rather one who suspects his faith in them and questions what he gathers from them."
The question your students should ask: If Ibn al-Haytham described the experimental method in 1011 CE, why do western histories of science credit Bacon in 1620? What does that gap tell us about how science history has been written?

973 – 1048 CECentral Asia / Persia
Eliminating observer bias — a medieval peer reviewer
Abu Rayhan al-Biruni
Al-Biruni was obsessed with measurement precision and the elimination of error. He insisted that the observer's own assumptions and cultural background could distort findings — and developed methods to counteract this. He travelled extensively, studied cultures from the inside rather than from assumptions, and demanded that measurements be repeatable and independent. His approach to India (Kitab al-Hind) is considered one of the first works of anthropological methodology — applying scientific standards to the study of human cultures.
Connection to modern science: Al-Biruni's concern about observer bias maps directly onto the modern concepts of blinding in clinical trials and independent replication. His instinct that the researcher's assumptions can corrupt results is foundational to modern scientific method.

980 – 1037 CEPersia
Clinical trials and evidence-based medicine
Ibn Sina — Avicenna
Ibn Sina's Canon of Medicine was the standard medical textbook in European universities until the 17th century. But more important than its conclusions was its method. Ibn Sina described what we would now call clinical trial methodology: test treatments on multiple patients, require that results be reproducible, distinguish the effect of a drug from the natural course of the disease, and test on humans not just animals. He also described contagion — the idea that disease can be transmitted through water and soil — centuries before germ theory.
"Medicine is the science by which we learn the various states of the human body in health and when not in health."
For your genetics unit: Modern genetic medicine — gene therapy, pharmacogenomics, CRISPR treatments — is evaluated through clinical trials. The methodology Ibn Sina described is the direct ancestor of how we test whether gene therapies actually work.

1258 – 1600 — transmission and the European Renaissance
1258 CEBaghdad
The fall of Baghdad — and the question of decline
Mongol invasion of the Abbasid Caliphate
The Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 destroyed the House of Wisdom and is often cited as the end of the Islamic Golden Age. But historians debate this. Some argue the decline had begun earlier — that a shift toward more conservative theological authority had already begun to constrain open inquiry. Others argue the Golden Age continued in different centres (Cairo, Samarkand, Moorish Spain) for centuries after. The question of why scientific leadership passed to Europe is genuinely contested and has no simple answer.
The question worth asking: What conditions allow science to flourish — and what causes it to stagnate? This is directly relevant to today: the same question applies to any society that must balance religious authority, political power and scientific freedom.

12th – 13th centuryEurope
European universities built on Islamic scholarship
Gerard of Cremona and the translation movement
When European universities emerged — Bologna (1088), Oxford (1096), Paris (1150) — their curricula were built substantially on Arabic texts. Gerard of Cremona alone translated over 87 works from Arabic into Latin, including works by al-Khwarizmi, Ibn Sina and al-Biruni, as well as Greek works preserved and extended by Islamic scholars. The European Scientific Revolution did not emerge from nowhere — it emerged from direct engagement with Islamic science, usually without acknowledgement.
What this means: The "western" scientific tradition is not purely western. It is a synthesis — built on Greek foundations, extended enormously by Islamic scholarship, then developed further in Europe. The story only makes sense as a connected global narrative.

1600 – present — the modern scientific system
1620England
Bacon formalises inductive method — building on what came before
Francis Bacon
Bacon's contribution was real — he articulated and popularised the experimental method at a moment when European science was ready to accelerate. But he did not invent it. Ibn al-Haytham had described it 600 years earlier. What Bacon did was codify it in a European context and make it influential in the emerging institutions of European science. His work was itself built on a tradition of Islamic scholarship transmitted through Latin universities.
The uncomfortable truth: Western histories of science typically begin with Bacon as the "founder" of the scientific method. This is historically inaccurate. It reflects how science history has been written — by the winners of a particular cultural moment — rather than what actually happened.

1934Vienna / London
Popper — falsifiability as the demarcation of science
Karl Popper
Popper's contribution was to provide the clearest philosophical articulation of what distinguishes science from non-science: a claim is scientific only if it makes predictions that could in principle be proven wrong. This solved Hume's problem of induction and gave the scientific community a clear standard. But it is worth noting: Ibn al-Haytham's practice, al-Biruni's methodology and Ibn Sina's clinical standards were all implicitly Popperian — they just didn't have the vocabulary.
"A theory that explains everything explains nothing."
For your students: Popper gave us the language. But the practice existed across multiple civilisations, centuries earlier. Science is a method that different human cultures have discovered independently — because it works.

TodayGlobal
Science is now a global, collaborative enterprise
The international scientific community
The Human Genome Project involved researchers from 20 countries across 6 continents. The COVID-19 vaccines were developed by teams spanning the US, UK, Germany, India and China — sharing data in real time. CRISPR was developed simultaneously in the US and by Emmanuelle Charpentier working across Sweden and France. Modern science is not western science — it is human science, and it is genuinely global. The institutions (peer review, replication, open publication) are the inheritance of all the traditions on this timeline.
The question to end on: If science is a universal human method — discovered independently by multiple civilisations — what does that tell us about its relationship to culture, religion and identity? Can science belong to everyone equally?

The question that connects everything: Ibn al-Haytham described the experimental method in 1011 CE. Bacon described it in 1620 CE. European universities were built on Arabic translations of Islamic science. Yet most science curricula credit the western tradition almost exclusively. Why — and what does that tell us about how knowledge and power interact? Does the method itself belong to any one culture?
